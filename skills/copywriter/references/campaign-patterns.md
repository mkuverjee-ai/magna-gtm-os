# Campaign patterns

Distilled from a store of 3,183 campaigns with live copy, 13,337 email steps, and 42,802 categorized replies. 809 of those campaigns landed at least one positive reply, the top one landed 147. These are patterns, not any one company's details. Use them for direction, then write from the specific prospect's pain.

All numbers below are measured from the corpus. Every example is genericized onto a fictional company. No real company's copy is reproduced.

## What the winners actually look like

- **Body length.** Across all copy the median email is 96 words. Across campaigns that earned five or more positive replies the median is 111 and the mean is 141. Read that carefully: winners that carry credible social proof run to 110 to 150 words, not 75. The tight 75 to 90 word discipline is for pure cold prospecting where you have no proof to lean on. When you add a real customer name or a real number, you earn the extra lines.
- **Sequence length.** Three steps is the single most common shape (about 1,200 campaigns). Four, five, and six step sequences are also common in the book. Two step is rarer (about 190). The house default stays two or three for cold prospecting. Longer sequences show up in warmer or event-driven plays.
- **Subject lines.** Median nine words. Half use spintax variation. A quarter are phrased as a question. Around one in five include "your."
- **Spintax everywhere.** 65 percent of bodies and 51 percent of subjects use `{{RANDOM| a | b | c }}` variation. At volume, varying the wording per send protects deliverability. Winning copy is written as a spun template, not a single fixed string.
- **Personalization.** 65 percent of bodies use a first-name variable. The strongest openers go further with a per-lead custom line (a specific idea, a specific observation), not just the name.

## The moves that recur in winners

- **Social proof, placed mid-body.** Half of all bodies (49 percent) pair a number with a named entity: "over 5,000 companies," "used by 300+ teams," a recognizable logo. It sits after the value proposition and before the ask, and it is what buys the longer word count.
- **The easy-out P.S.** 27 percent of emails end with a P.S. that hands the prospect a graceful exit: "If this isn't relevant, just tell me and I'll stop." It lowers the cost of replying, and a reply is a reply.
- **The soft-question CTA.** The winning ask is a question, not a demand. "Worth a quick look?" "Would it help if I sent it over?" One ask, phrased as a low-friction yes or no.
- **The multiple-choice breakup.** The strongest breakup emails offer the prospect a menu: "Reply with one: not the right fit / bad timing / need more details." It converts the silent procrastinators by making the reply a single tap.
- **The value-first opener.** The best first emails lead with something true and specific about the prospect (a custom idea, an observation about how they operate), then the wedge. Generic openers underperform.

## Reply reality (from 42,802 categorized replies)

The single largest reply bucket is out-of-office, then role changes and opt-outs. Genuine positive intent (interested, meeting request, information request) is a minority of replies but is the only bucket that matters. Judge copy on those, never on opens. This is also why the QC pass matters: a merged field that failed silently poisons the one line that earns the positive.

## Benchmark reply rates

A curated benchmark set inside the store carries measured reply rates from about 0.9 percent to 11.1 percent, averaging around 5.4 percent, across hook types (product launch, event invite, webinar invite, follow-up, reactivation). Treat mid single digits as a healthy cold-outbound reply rate and anything approaching double digits as a strong performer worth studying.

## What loses

- **Generic openers** that could go to anyone. If the first line is not true about this specific prospect, rewrite it.
- **A single fixed string at scale.** No spintax means identical sends, which hurts placement.
- **Complex language for simple audiences.** For non-native English or non-technical buyers, plain and short wins.
- **Stacked CTAs.** One ask per email. Two halves the odds of either.
- **Length without proof.** The winners earn their extra words with a real number or a real name. Long copy with nothing to back it just gets skimmed.

## Reconciling with the house rules

The `SKILL.md` hard rules (75 to 90 words, sentences under 20 words, two or three steps, no em dashes) are the discipline for cold prospecting, and they hold. The corpus shows where the best campaigns flex them: more words when there is real social proof, more steps for warmer or event-driven plays. Flex on purpose, with a reason, not by accident.
